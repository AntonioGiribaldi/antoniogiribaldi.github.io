  • Bidding-up Treasuries: a New Theory for Asset Purchase Programs
    Leggi il Paper
    Show abstract

    I propose a new macroeconomic theory that describes how asset purchase programs implemented by central banks or governments affect asset prices and generate real economic effects. I define an asset purchase program as a government commitment to purchase Treasury bonds at an overpriced value. When the government announces the policy, financial markets price in the opportunity to sell these overpriced bonds to the government, driving bond prices up. First, I provide causal empirical evidence of a price bust in eligible Treasury bonds following Fed purchases, consistent with the hypothesis that bond prices were incorporating this resale opportunity. I then propose two theoretical frameworks for asset purchase programs. In the first theory, the government affects bond prices by committing to purchase them above the market price. In the second theory, the government issues infinitely lived bonds; by promising to reduce their supply to zero in the limit, the government induces a bubbly equilibrium, leading financial markets to price a positive bubbly component into bond prices upon announcement. For both theoretical models, I analyze optimal asset purchase programs and identify a novel policy trade-off. On the one hand, the policy is costly because the government must finance the capital gains accruing to bondholders. On the other hand, elevated bond prices allow the government to raise more debt revenue for a given volume of issuance, thereby circumventing potentially binding debt limits and facilitating tax smoothing.
